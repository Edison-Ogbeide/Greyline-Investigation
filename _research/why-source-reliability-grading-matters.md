### A source is not a finding.

Every investigation produces material of wildly varying quality. A signed court judgment and an anonymous forum post might both mention the same name, but they are not the same kind of evidence, and a report that treats them identically has already failed the client reading it.

This is the problem source reliability grading exists to solve. It is not a formality. It is the mechanism that stops a weak signal from being dressed up as a strong one.

<hr class="gold-rule">

### The Admiralty Code, Applied

Greyline grades sources using a modified version of the **NATO Admiralty Code**, a two-axis system originally developed for military intelligence assessment and now widely used across the private and government intelligence sectors.

Every source is scored on:

- **Reliability of the source itself** (graded A to F): is this a source that has consistently proven trustworthy, or one with no track record either way?
- **Credibility of the specific information** (graded 1 to 6): independent of the source's general reliability, how likely is this particular piece of information to be true?

The two axes are scored separately because they answer different questions. A generally reliable source can still report something dubious. A source with no track record can still produce something independently verifiable. Collapsing the two into a single "trust score" loses exactly the distinction that matters.

A source graded **A1** (completely reliable, confirmed) supports a stated fact. A source graded **E5** (unreliable, improbable) does not belong in a finding at all, no matter how convenient it would be to include it.

<hr class="gold-rule">

### What This Looks Like in a Report

Grading is not an internal exercise that stays in an analyst's notebook. Where it is material to a conclusion, the grade is disclosed in the report itself.

In practice, this means a Greyline report distinguishes between three different things that a less disciplined report would present as equivalent:

1. **A corroborated fact**: independently confirmed by at least one further source, stated plainly.
2. **A single-source claim**: reported as what it is, a claim from one source, not yet corroborated, with the grading attached so the reader can weigh it themselves.
3. **An analyst's assessment**: the analyst's own reasoned judgement about what the corroborated facts mean, clearly separated from the facts and labelled as interpretation.

A client, a solicitor, or a court reading a Greyline report can see not just what was found, but how much weight the finding can bear. That distinction is the entire point of grading, and it is why our full breakdown of finding classification lives on the <a href="{{ '/method/analysis/' | relative_url }}">Analysis</a> page of our published method.

### Why This Is Harder Than It Sounds

Grading disciplines are easy to state and easy to abandon under pressure. A tight deadline, a client who wants a stronger answer than the evidence supports, or a source that is inconvenient to downgrade all create pressure to round up.

The discipline only holds if it is applied the same way regardless of what the client wants to hear. A clean finding is reported as a clean finding. An unresolved question is reported as unresolved. Grading a weak source generously because the conclusion would otherwise be less satisfying is not analysis, it is storytelling with a badge of rigour attached.

This is also why grading has to be recorded contemporaneously, in the working notes, at the point the source is used, not reconstructed afterwards to justify a conclusion already reached.

<hr class="gold-rule">

### What This Means in Practice

If you are instructing an investigator, whether Greyline or anyone else, the questions worth asking are simple: does this firm grade its sources at all, is that grading visible in the final report, and does a single-source claim look different on the page from a corroborated fact?

If the answer to any of those is no, the report may still be accurate. But you have no way to tell the difference between a well-supported conclusion and a confident guess, and that is precisely the gap source reliability grading is designed to close.
